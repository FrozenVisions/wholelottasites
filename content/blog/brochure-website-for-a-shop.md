A brochure website for a shop is a page a customer reads before they travel. It is not a checkout. It says when you are open, what you stock, where the door is, and how to ask. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This is for an independent shop. I don’t run one, and I won’t invent a range or a review.

Whether you need a site at all is covered in [does a shop need a website](/blog/does-a-shop-need-a-website/). This post is the contents of a non-ecommerce site, for an owner who wants people to visit. What I build for that is on [shop website design](/websites-for/shops/).

## What a brochure website for a shop includes

A brochure site is for a visit, not an order. A simple website for a small shop is usually home, what you sell, how to find the door, a short note on who runs it, and contact. You supply the words and the photos.

### Opening hours

Write the hours in text, including the days you are closed. Add bank holidays and stocktake days to that list. Use the same hours on your Google Business Profile. If they disagree, the customer has to guess. Change both on the day they change.

### Where the shop is

Put the address in text, then a map. Name the town. A website for a local shop that only says “the high street” could be anywhere. Mention a step, or say there is no step-free entrance. If you are down a side street, upstairs, or through a yard, describe the door, and say where to park. Put the pin on the entrance people walk through.

### What you stock

Name the categories in sentences. Key cutting, cards beside the gifts, alterations at a clothes shop: that line is often the reason for the trip. Drop anything you no longer sell, and name the months for anything seasonal. Skip the supplier’s whole catalogue. “Independent greetings cards, wrapping and a small toy shelf” tells someone whether to come.

### Photos

Use photos of your shelves, window or frontage, and only when you may use them and anyone identifiable has agreed. Don’t autoplay a video. The hours and the address still belong in text.

### How to get in touch

Put the phone number in real text, large enough to tap. A number inside a logo will not dial. Add an email you actually read. A short form is enough for “do you have this?” or a hold, if that is how you already work. Only promise a reply time you will keep.

A shop website needs one next step on the home page: call, visit, or ask.

## Read it on a phone

Read the pages on your phone before you share the address. The hours, the address and the number should be readable without pinching, and the number should dial. Serve the site over HTTPS.

## When you do not need a basket

If people pay at the till, say “We don’t sell online.” That is more useful than a button that goes nowhere. A few payment links, for a deposit or a small set of products, can wait until you have your own Stripe account. You set Stripe up. I connect the links. The card machine stays with your current provider. A marketplace listing can stay too. It is not a stand-in for the hours and the address.

A large catalogue, with sizes, stock levels and postage, is a different job, quoted from around £1,200. I’ll say so before you pay. Add it only if you will pack the orders, keep the stock figure honest when the same item sells in the shop that day, and handle returns. If not, keep the brochure.

## Before you publish

1. Are the hours in text, including closed days and the next bank holiday you already know?
2. Is the address in text, with the right door, parking or a step?
3. Do the categories match what you stock now, with photos of your own shop?
4. Does the number dial, and does a test message reach you?
5. Do the website and the Google listing match, with a link each way?
6. If you don’t sell online, does the page say so?
7. Can you read it on a phone, over HTTPS, without pinching?
