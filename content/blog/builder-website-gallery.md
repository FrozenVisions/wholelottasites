## What a builder website gallery should show

Show work you did, on homes you have permission to show. Customers want to see finished work before they invite you round to quote. I will not put another firm’s project, or a stock photo of a site you never worked on, on your page.

Group the photos by the jobs you still take. Use the names customers use: an extension, a loft conversion, a kitchen, a renovation, groundwork. Leave off a type you do not offer. A single pile labelled “building work” is harder to scan than a short group for each job you actually do.

For each job, aim for three kinds of picture when you have them.

1. **The start.** The room, the plot or the wall as you found it, in daylight.
2. **The work.** A clear shot of a stage that explains the job, such as steels going in or a roof opened up. Skip a blurry frame from a dark loft.
3. **The finish.** A view close to the start shot, once the job is done and the space is tidy.

Phone photos in good light are enough. Stand far enough back that the whole wall, or the whole extension, is in the frame. A close-up of one socket does not show the job.

Ask before you publish a customer’s home. If they say no, leave that job out. If they say yes to the outside and no to the bedrooms, follow that. Take a photo down later if they change their mind.

## Before and after

A before-and-after pair is two photos of the same job, so a customer can see what changed. The useful pair is the same corner, or the same side of the house, at the start and at the end. If you had to move the camera, say so in the caption.

Label each picture. “Before” or “After” as the first word is enough. Google’s Business Profile help page says a photo should be in focus and well lit, with no significant alterations or excessive filters, so the image represents the real place. Use that standard on the website too. The person who books you will stand in that room.

You do not need a pair for every job. A finished photo on its own is still worth showing. Do not use a “before” from a different house.

## Captions with the job type and the town

Put a line of text next to the photo, or directly under it. Google’s image guidance says the words beside a picture, including the caption, help it understand what the photo shows. That does not promise a place in the results.

Write the job and the town where you did it. For example: “After: single-storey extension, Mansfield.” If the job was in Derby, write Derby. If you cover Nottingham and you will not travel to Leicester, do not add Leicester to make the line longer.

Keep the caption to the job, the town and, where it helps, one plain detail: a rear extension, a dormer, a new kitchen in an existing house. The list of towns you will travel to belongs on the builder page linked above, not crammed into every caption. [Do trades in Derby and Leicester still need a website in 2026?](/blog/do-trades-in-derby-and-leicester-need-a-website/) is the wider note on those two cities.

A short file name helps in the same modest way. Google’s image guidance uses `my-new-black-kitten.jpg` as a useful name, and `IMG00023.JPG` as a weak one. `extension-mansfield-after.jpg` is the shape to aim for. That is an example of a file name, not a job I am saying you completed.

## File size, so the gallery stays quick

Google’s image guidance says images are often the largest part of a page, and that can make the page slow and expensive to load. Sharp photos are still worth keeping. Blurry ones are a poor substitute. I crop and compress each picture for the site so the gallery stays readable on a phone.

Put each photo in the page as a normal image, with its own address. Google’s image guidance says it can find images used that way. It also says it does not index an image that exists only as a CSS background. A picture buried in the design is a weak way to show a job.

JPEG, PNG and WebP are among the formats Google Search says it can read in that image address. The photos you also add to Google Business Profile need the format on Google’s help page, in the next section.

I am not setting a single kilobyte target in this post. I have not measured one number that fits every photo. Open the gallery on a phone. The pictures should appear without a long wait, and the words should stay readable without pinching.

## Alt text on every job photo

Alt text is the written description stored on the image. A person using a screen reader hears it. Google’s image guidance calls alt text the most important extra detail you can give a photo, and says Google uses it together with the words on the page. The same page says filling the alt text with repeated keywords makes a poor experience and can look like spam.

Write one sentence about what is in the frame. For example: “After photo of a loft conversion in Nottingham, with the new stairs and a roof window.” Change the sentence when the photo changes. Do not paste “builder website gallery” into every picture. The caption can carry the job and the town. The alt text should still make sense if the picture fails to load.

## The same photos on Google Business Profile

The gallery on your website does not, by itself, become the photos on Google Search and Maps. You add those on the Business Profile. Google’s help page says your photos and videos show on Google only after you verify the profile.

On a computer, the steps on that page are: open Google Search or Maps, search for the business name and city, open Photos, then Add photos. You can choose more than one. Your newest upload appears first among the media you added. A photo can stay Pending while it is processed, or while the profile is not verified. Pending media does not show to customers. Not approved does not show either. Live means customers can see it. Google says this can take up to 24 to 48 hours.

Photos look best, on that same help page, when they are:

- JPG or PNG
- Between 10 KB and 5 MB
- At least 250 pixels wide and 250 pixels tall
- Recommended resolution 720 pixels wide and 720 pixels tall
- In focus, well lit, and without significant alterations or excessive filters

Use the same shoot for the website and the listing. The listing file needs to sit inside those limits. The website file should be the compressed copy. A logo and a cover photo are separate from the job photos. Google says the cover you pick is not guaranteed to be the first image customers see.

Upload photos of work you did. Add an exterior only when it is a real one you are allowed to show: a yard, a unit, or the front of a job. Skip a picture of a building that is not yours.

The website link on the listing should open your site on https, so someone can reach the gallery. The name, the phone number and the area should match the site. That check is in [your Google Business Profile and your website](/blog/google-business-profile-and-website/). You stay the owner. I will not ask for the Google account password.

## Quick answers

**What should a builder website gallery include?**
Your own jobs, with permission. Show the start and the finish when you have both, group them by the work you still do, and caption each photo with the job and the town.

**How large should the photos be?**
On the website, keep them sharp and light enough that the gallery opens on a phone. Google’s image guidance says images are often the largest part of a page. For Google Business Profile, the help page says photos look best as JPG or PNG, between 10 KB and 5 MB, at least 250 pixels wide and tall, with 720 by 720 recommended.

**Do website photos appear on Google Maps on their own?**
No. Add them on the Business Profile after it is verified. Google says they can take up to 24 to 48 hours to show. The website link on the listing is a separate step.

**Will a gallery get the business to the top of Google?**
No. I don’t promise a position on Google, a place on Maps, or a number of leads.
