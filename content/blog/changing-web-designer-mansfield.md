## Changing web designer in Mansfield: who owns the domain, hosting and logins

Write this list down while the current designer still replies. Each line is a separate login. Losing one of them is how a simple handover turns into a site you cannot move.

- **The domain.** The name, such as yourbusiness.co.uk, is the address. The website is the pages at that address. You, or your company, should be the registrant. A designer does not need to own the name to build the site. Choosing and registering one is covered in [choosing a domain name for a small business](/blog/domain-name-for-a-small-business/).
- **DNS.** These records point the name at the host and at email. They often live in the registrar account. Sometimes they live at the host. You need a login that can edit them.
- **Hosting.** This is where the files live, and a database if the site has one.
- **The builder.** WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, or a custom site. That login edits the pages. It is not the domain login.
- **Email on the domain.** Forwarding, or a mailbox, stops if the mail records are dropped while the website is moved.
- **Google Business Profile.** You should be the owner. Anyone else should be a manager you can remove. The website link on the listing is what people tap.
- **Search Console.** This is Google’s free tool for site owners. Ask to be added as an owner. If the site has Analytics, that is another login. Neither one is a ranking report.

## Getting access back

Email the current designer. Ask for five things, and keep the reply.

1. The registrar, the account email, the renewal month, and either a login in your name or a transfer into a registrar account you control.
2. DNS access, if it sits somewhere else.
3. A copy of the site: the words, the photos you supplied, and a list of every public address.
4. Hosting access, or an export of the files, if the next site needs them.
5. Your Google account added as an owner on the Google Business Profile and in Search Console.

When you are in, change the password and the recovery email to ones only you hold. If the registrar offers an extra sign-in check, turn it on with your own phone.

For a .uk name the registry is Nominet, and you still manage the name at a registrar. A .uk name moves between registrars by changing the IPS tag to the new registrar’s tag. A .com usually needs the domain unlocked and an authorisation code from the current registrar. Follow the steps in that account. I am not the registrar, and I will not guess their fee.

If nobody replies, contact the registrar named on the domain. Take proof you trade under that name: invoices, the name on the van, and a Companies House number if you have one. The registrar decides whether to reset the account. I cannot promise that they will. I am not a solicitor, and this is not advice on your contract with the old designer.

DNS access is enough to point the name at a new host. The registrar transfer can happen after that. Copy every DNS record, including the mail records, before anyone edits them. Miss those records and the new site can load while email at the domain stops.

Keep the old site up until the new one is live, the important old addresses have been tested, and you have your own copy of the words and photos.

## Keep the page addresses, or 301 the ones that change

Google has already fetched particular addresses: the homepage, a service, a town, the contact page. An address that stays the same still opens the right page for anyone who saved it. An address that changes, with nothing behind it, becomes an error.

A **301 redirect** is how a server says an address has permanently moved, and sends the person to the new one. Browsers and Google both read that response. A sentence on the homepage that says the page has moved is not a 301. A link in the menu is not one either.

List the addresses before the new design starts.

- Open every page in the menu and copy the full address.
- Save the old sitemap if the site has one, often at `/sitemap.xml`.
- Search Google for `site:yourdomain.co.uk`, with your real domain in place of that example. The results are addresses Google has seen. They are not a ranking, and they are not a full history of the site.

Keep a path on the new site when the page is still the right page. `/contact` can stay `/contact`. A page about the work you still do can keep its path. Pages that are no longer true should come off. Sorting that list is the job in [how to update an old website](/blog/update-my-old-website/).

When a path has to change, set one 301 from that old address to the closest new page. Do the whole list, not only the homepage. Someone who saved the electrical repairs page should land on electrical repairs if that page still exists. If the new site has no page about that old subject, leave the error. Sending every retired address to the homepage hides the gap and dumps the person in the wrong place.

Use one public version of the name. Serve the site on https, and on either www or the bare name. Redirect the other versions to that one. Print that version on the van, the quote and the Google listing.

Build on a preview address first, so the current site stays on the real domain. Add the redirects before the domain points at the new site. Then open an old address in a private window. You should arrive on the new page. Leave the redirects in place. Removing them later makes those old addresses errors again.

## Search Console

Search Console shows pages Google has indexed, and addresses it reports as not found. It is free. It does not guarantee a position, visits or enquiries.

Ask the current designer to add your Google account as an owner before they go. If that does not happen, verify it yourself once you control DNS, or once you can place a file or a tag on the new site. Verification tells Google that the account controls the site. It does not move you up the results.

After launch, look for addresses reported as not found. Where an old page should still lead somewhere, add the 301, then look again. Submit the new sitemap if the site has one. You can also inspect an important URL and see whether Google can fetch it.

Search Console includes a change-of-address option for a move to a different domain name. Leave it alone when the domain stays the same. Use it only if the name on the van really has to change, the 301 redirects from the old name are already in place, and you have verified both properties. Keeping the current domain is the better default. People, quotes and any existing links already use it. ## A switch-over checklist

Tick a line when you have seen it, not because someone said it was done.

1. List the registrar, DNS, hosting, the builder, email, Google Business Profile, Search Console and Analytics. Write down which email can reset each one.
2. Check the registrant. It should be you or the company. If it is the old designer, ask for the name to be put in your name while they still reply. Note the renewal month.
3. Save the current words, your photos, and every public URL. Keep the menu list and the `site:` search together.
4. Copy the DNS records, including mail, before anyone changes them.
5. Agree the new pages. Keep an address where the page is still true. Write one 301 for every address that will change, aimed at the closest new page.
6. Build on a preview address. On a phone, the number should tap through, the form should arrive to you, and the address bar should show a secure connection.
7. Turn the redirects on, then point the domain. Test old addresses in a private window.
8. Tap the Google Business Profile website link. Update it if it misses the new site. Remove access the old designer no longer needs.
9. Confirm you are an owner in Search Console. In the weeks after launch, redirect any old address that still matters and now shows as not found.
10. Change the passwords you were handed. The registrar recovery email should be yours.

## Quick answers

**Does changing web designer in Mansfield keep my Google rankings?**
No. Nobody can guarantee a position, a number of visits, or any enquiries. Keep the domain if you can, keep each page address that is still right, and use a 301 redirect when an address has to change. That tells Google the page has moved. It is not a promise the page will sit in the same place.

**Who should own the domain and the logins?**
You, or your company. Get the registrar login, or a transfer into an account you control, before the old designer stops replying. DNS access is enough to point the name at a new host. Copy the mail records before you change DNS.

**What does a 301 redirect do?**
It is a permanent redirect from the server. The old address sends the person to the new page and says the move is permanent. Point each old address at the closest matching page, test it, and leave the redirect in place.

**Do I need the Search Console change of address?**
Only when the domain name itself changes, and only after both addresses are verified and the 301 redirects work. When the domain stays the same, confirm you are an owner and check for addresses reported as not found.
