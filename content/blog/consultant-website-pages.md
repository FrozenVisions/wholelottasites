What to put on a consultant website is a short list. The reader is often on a phone, after searching for the problem or being given your name. They need to know who you help, what the work involves, and how to start. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield, in the East Midlands. This is for the consultant. I don’t do the consulting.

The pages for this work are on [consultant website design](/websites-for/consultants/). A site that is mainly project examples sits closer to [freelancer websites](/websites-for/freelancers/). The wider checklist is [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## Say who you help

Write one sentence a stranger can repeat. Name the person or organisation, and the situation they are in. “I help independent shops get their books ready for the accountant” is useful. “Bespoke strategic solutions” could sit on any site.

Add who you do not take: a size of organisation, a sector, or a job that is not yours. That saves a call that was never going to fit.

People type the problem, or your name after a recommendation. Use those words on the first screen and in the page title. “Home” is a weak title. “Planning help for householders in [the town]” is fair when that is the page and the town is yours. Name the towns if you visit, say if you work across the UK, or say if you only meet by video.

## Name the work in the client’s words

List the engagements you actually take, and leave off work you might add later. For each one, write what happens in the first few weeks, and what you need from them: figures, a visit, access to a system, or a written brief. If someone else does the implementation, say so, so a client does not discover that on the invoice.

Publish a fee only when it is the one you charge: a day rate, a fixed project, or a retainer. If it depends on the job, say what the email should include and when a figure will come back. Don’t invent a rate to fill a gap.

## Explain the first conversation

Say whether the first call is free, how long it lasts, and what it covers. Put that beside the button. A short form is enough for many consultants: name, organisation, email, the problem in a sentence or two, when they want to start, and any deadline. A phone number is optional. If you publish one, use real text, large enough to tap. A number inside a logo cannot be dialled.

Say what happens next: a written scope, a proposal, or a clear no. Name what sits outside a normal engagement, such as weekend work. Only promise a reply time you will keep. A form is a request. It holds a slot only when a booking system you already use does that. I’ll link that system. I don’t sell a separate one.

## About you, without borrowed proof

A qualification belongs on the page only when you hold it. Don’t add a badge for a scheme you are not in. I won’t invent a registration, an insurance line, or a count of years.

A case study is a real engagement you are allowed to describe, in your words. If you cannot name the client, describe the kind of work and leave the name, the logo and any figure off. I will not invent a client, a result, a quote or a logo. Link to your Google reviews. Don’t paste them onto the site.

Use the same name on the site, your email and your [Google Business Profile](/google-business-profile/) if you have one. If you work from home and do not want the street published, say so. You stay the owner. I can’t promise where the listing appears. Setup or a fix of that profile is £149 on its own, and it is included with a founding website.

## Five pages

1. **Home.** Who you help, where you work, and one way to start.
2. **Services.** Each engagement, and what you need from the client.
3. **How it works.** The first conversation, the fee or how to ask, and what happens next.
4. **About.** Only the facts you can stand behind.
5. **Contact.** The form, an email you read, and a phone number if you want one.

A blog can wait until you will write it. A client login, a large library or several languages is quoted from around £1,200. Read the first screen on a phone before you publish, over HTTPS, without pinching to zoom. If the current site fails that, see [what to do if your website is not mobile friendly](/blog/website-not-mobile-friendly/).
