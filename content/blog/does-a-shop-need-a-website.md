Does a shop need a website? A customer often checks three things before they leave the house: whether you are open, whether you are likely to stock the thing, and how to find the door. A website is one place those answers can sit in ordinary text, on a phone, at an address you control. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This is for the owner of a local shop. I don’t run one.

A full online shop, with live stock, a basket and shipping rules, is a different project. Many independent shops never need that. They need the hours, the range and the visit. ## An address you control

A Facebook page, an Instagram grid or a marketplace listing can still earn its place. None of those is an address you own. A domain such as yourshop.co.uk is the address, not the website. With a new website we cover the first year of a standard domain, and it’s registered in your name. Premium domains cost extra. From year two you pay the domain renewal and hosting, or monthly care.

Hours left in a comment get buried. A photo of the window does not say whether you close on Monday, or whether there is a step at the door. Your own page is the link you can put on the bag, the window and your Google listing.

## Three answers, in text

Write them so a phone can show them without pinching to zoom.

1. **Whether you are open.** Put the hours in text, including closed days. Add bank holidays and stocktake days before people travel to a locked door. Use the same hours on your Google Business Profile. If the two copies disagree, a customer cannot tell which to trust.
2. **Whether you sell it.** Name the categories in real text, not one photo of the window. Drop lines you no longer stock. If you don’t sell online, say so. People would rather read that than hunt for a basket that isn’t there.
3. **How to find the door.** The address in text, a note on parking, and whether there is a step. If you are down a side street or above another shop, describe the entrance. Put the phone number in real text, large enough to tap. A number inside a logo cannot be dialled.

A short form covers “do you have this?” or a request to hold an item, if that is how you already work. You reply. Live stock is a system behind the page, and it is not part of a small shop site. Use photos of your own shelves or frontage. Give the page its own title. “Home” is a weak one. I can’t promise where it will appear.

## A short site is often enough

Does a shop need a website when most of the trade is through the door? The page still earns its place: hours, range and address, so someone can decide before they travel.

A few orders or deposits can use payment links from your own Stripe account. You set Stripe up. I connect the links. A large catalogue, with stock levels and shipping rules, is quoted separately, from around £1,200. I’ll say so before you pay if that is the job.

Leave a blog until you will write it. Five pages are enough for most shops: home, what you sell, how to visit, a short about, and contact. Serve the site over HTTPS, and read it on your own phone before you share the address.
