What to put on an electrician’s website is a short list. The reader is often on a phone, with the lights out, a board that keeps tripping, or a landlord who needs a safety report. They need to know you cover their area, that you do this job, and how to reach you. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This checklist is about the pages and the wording. I don’t do the electrical work.

The pages for this trade are on [websites for electricians](/websites-for/electricians/). See also [what to put on a plumber’s website](/blog/plumber-website-checklist/) and [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## Make the phone number easy to tap

Put the number in real text on every page, large enough to tap. A number inside a logo, or in a photo of the van, cannot be dialled. Use the same number on the van, on invoices and on your [Google Business Profile](/google-business-profile/).

State the hours, including emergency call-outs, and say if you finish at six or leave weekends alone. A fuse board upgrade or a landlord’s report can wait for a form. A house with no power still needs one tap. If that fault belongs to the network operator, say so.

## Name the jobs in the customer’s words

People type the problem, not the slogan on the van. Name only the work you do.

- **Emergency and faults.** No power, a board that trips, a socket that sparked. Say when you attend.
- **Planned work in the home.** Extra sockets, lighting, outdoor electrics, a consumer unit (the fuse board) upgrade, and a rewire.
- **EV chargers**, if you install them.
- **Landlords.** An electrical installation condition report (an EICR).

An EICR is the written report a landlord or a buyer often asks for. A certificate after new work says what was installed. Use the name you issue. Leave out solar, alarms or commercial work unless you take it.

## Write the towns and the hours in full

List the towns and villages you travel to. “The local area” does not say whether you will come. If you stop at a distance, say so. I can’t promise a position for “electrician near me”, or for any other phrase.

Put the hours next to the towns. Mention a night or weekend call-out fee only when you charge one, and publish a price only when it is real. If you work from home and do not want the street published, say that you visit the customer. There is a shorter note for [trades in Derby and Leicester](/blog/do-trades-in-derby-and-leicester-need-a-website/).

## Show what a customer can check

Say who turns up. A daylight photo of you and the van is enough. Show finished work only with permission, and only when it was yours.

If you are in a scheme such as NICEIC or NAPIT, or another you actually belong to, put the name and number in text and link the public register. Do the same for insurance you hold. I will not add a badge you have not earned. Say whether you self-certify domestic work or notify building control, matching where you work. I will not write the regulations for you.

Link your Google reviews. Do not paste borrowed ratings or invented quotes. You stay the owner of the listing. Setup or a fix is £149 on its own, and it is included with a founding website. Optional management is £99–£150 a month. I can’t promise where the listing appears.

## Five pages, and a form for quieter jobs

The form is for work that can wait: a name, a phone number or email, the town or postcode, the job, whether it is a house, a flat or a rental, and a photo of the fuse board. Keep the number above the form.

1. **Home.** You are an electrician, the area, and the number.
2. **Services**, split as above. A landlords page when that work is steady.
3. **Towns**, on their own page or in a clear section.
4. **About**, with the details a customer can check.
5. **Contact**, with the form, the number and the email.

“Home” is a weak page title. “Fuse board upgrade in [the town]” is fair when that is the work and the town. [A website built from scratch](/website-built-from-scratch/) can start from the details above.
