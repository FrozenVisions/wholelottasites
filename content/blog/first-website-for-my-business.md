A first website for my business is what people type when the work has started and there is still nowhere to send a customer. The name might only be on a Facebook page, a van, or a stack of cards. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This is for the owner of a new UK small business or trade. I don’t do the plumbing, the decorating or the consulting.

You do not need a logo first. You need the name customers will recognise, what you do, where, and how to get in touch. [A website built from scratch](/website-built-from-scratch/) is that job.

## What a first website for my business has to do

Someone who finds you, often on a phone, should know three things without hunting.

1. What you do, in words a customer would use. “Boiler repair and bathroom fitting” beats a slogan that could sit on any van.
2. Where. The towns you travel to, or the address, hours and parking if people come to you. “The local area” does not say whether you will come.
3. How to reach you. A phone number in real text, large enough to tap, an email you read, and a form that arrives with you. A number drawn inside a logo cannot be dialled.

Write that as text, not as a photo of a flyer. Put the same name, phone and address on your Google Business Profile. The profile is free to set up. I can’t promise where the listing appears. If the two copies disagree, a customer cannot tell which to trust.

Use the trading name you will put on quotes. If you already have a company number, it can go on the about page. I won’t invent a registration, an insurance line or a review.

## What to put on a first website for my business

Four pages are enough for most new businesses. The longer list is [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

- **Home.** What you do, where, and a number or enquiry button on the first screen.
- **Services.** Name each job you will actually do. Leave off work you might take one day.
- **About.** Who turns up. Show a registration a customer can check, such as a Gas Safe number that is yours. Don’t add a badge for a scheme you are not in.
- **Contact.** Number, email, form, and the towns or the address. Ask for the job, the town or postcode, and whether it is urgent. Only promise a reply time you will keep.

Add a gallery only with photos of your own jobs. Daylight phone photos are enough. Ask before you show someone else’s home or face. If you have none yet, launch on the words rather than stock pictures of a different workshop.

A call-out trade should name the jobs and the towns. Notes for that sit on the pages for [plumbers](/websites-for/plumbers/), [electricians](/websites-for/electricians/), [builders](/websites-for/builders/), [decorators](/websites-for/decorators/), [cleaners](/websites-for/cleaners/) and [gardeners](/websites-for/gardeners/). A consultant, a freelancer or a shop that has not opened yet should start from [websites for small businesses](/websites-for/) and open the matching page. Use it as a contents list. Don’t paste it.

Give each page its own title and a short description. “Home” is a weak title. “Electrician in [the town]” is fair when that is the page and the town is yours. Serve the site over HTTPS, and read it on a phone before you publish. Buttons should be easy to tap, and the text readable without pinching to zoom.

## What to send, and what can wait

You do not need a long brief.

1. The business name, and your name if customers deal with you.
2. Two or three sentences on the work, as you would say them on the phone.
3. The towns, or the address and hours. If you work by appointment, say so. Don’t invent hours you will not keep.
4. A phone number and an email you will answer. An inbox you check beats an address on your domain that nobody reads.
5. Photos, if you have any you are happy to publish.
6. A domain, if you own one. If you don’t, buy it in your own name, such as yourbusiness.co.uk, after you check the spelling. The domain is the address, not the website. I can help connect it. The account stays yours. Avoid a name customers will mix up with another local firm.

Leave these until you need them: a blog you will not write, booking before you have a system, a shop with stock and shipping, card payments before your own Stripe account, and pages that repeat the homepage. Facebook or Instagram can stay. Neither is an address you control, and a free subdomain is a poor link on a van.
