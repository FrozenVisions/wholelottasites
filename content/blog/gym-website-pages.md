What to put on a gym website is a short list. The reader is usually on a phone, comparing a gym, a PT studio or a class. They need this week’s timetable, a price or a way to ask, and how to try a session. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This is for the owner. I don’t run a gym.

The pages for this work are on [gym website design](/websites-for/gyms/). A trainer on their own usually needs fewer pages than a floor with classes all day. The wider checklist is [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## Put the timetable in real text

Write each class as text: name, day, start time, length, and who it is for. Say if it is for beginners, members only, or not meant for a first visit. A photo of the whiteboard, or a PDF you have to pinch, fails on a phone.

Opening hours and class times are not the same. The building can open at 6am while the first class is mid-morning. Write both, and change the line when a class moves or ends. If the week changes often, link the system you already use and name the regular sessions beside it.

## Membership prices, or how to ask

Publish a price only when it is the one you charge. Set out monthly, a minimum term, a class pack, pay as you go, off-peak, a concession and any joining fee. List only the options you sell. When the price changes, change the page. If you would rather not show a figure, say how to ask. Don’t invent one.

Put the notice period, any freeze rule and what a trial includes in the same words as the agreement. A direct debit, a door fob or a class app stays with the provider you already use. I’ll link to it. I don’t rebuild it.

## Location and opening hours

Put the address in text, with the entrance, parking and any step at the door. A trainer who visits clients should list the towns, not a home address. If you hire a room in another gym, say whether a new client needs a day pass.

Say if the floor stays open when reception is shut. Use the same hours on the home page and the contact page, and add bank holidays when you know them.

## Trial and booking

Make one next step obvious: book a trial, book an induction, or enquire. Put it on the home page, beside the timetable and on the contact page. Link the booking system you already use, or use a short form: name, phone or email, the class or the goal, and a preferred day. A form is a request, not a held place, unless the system holds one. Keep a phone number above it, in text large enough to tap.

Beside the button, state any induction, what to bring, an age limit, and whether the floor can be used without a booking.

## Coaches and facilities

Name who teaches, and what they teach. Write a qualification only when that coach holds it. Don’t add a badge for a scheme they are not in. Use a photo only with their permission.

List the facilities you actually have, such as free weights, a studio, changing rooms, showers, lockers, parking or a step-free entrance. Use your own photos, and ask before you show a member.

## Contact, and your Google Business Profile

The contact page needs the number, an email you read, the form or booking link, and the address.

On your Google Business Profile, use the same name, address, phone and hours as the website. Link the website from the profile, and link the profile from the website, so a visitor can open the map. If the profile has an appointment link, point it at the same trial or booking page. Don’t copy reviews onto the site, and don’t borrow a rating. I can’t promise where the listing appears.

## Read it on a phone first

Check the timetable on a phone before you publish. Class times should be readable without pinching to zoom, the trial button should be easy to tap, and the number should dial. Serve the pages over HTTPS. “Home” is a weak title. “Gym classes in [the town]” is fair when that is the page and the town is yours. If the current site fails on a phone, see [make my website mobile friendly](/mobile-friendly-website/).

## What to put on a gym website, page by page

Most gyms and class studios need these. A personal trainer can stop at home, what you offer, where you train, and contact.

1. Home: the name, the town, today’s hours, and a way to try a class or join.
2. The timetable, in text.
3. Memberships or prices, or how to ask.
4. Coaches.
5. Facilities and how to get in, if the home page does not already say.
6. Contact: number, email, address and the booking link.

Before any design, gather the timetable, the prices you will publish, the hours, the address, the trial rules, the coach names and a few photos you can use. [A website built from scratch](/website-built-from-scratch/) can start from the business name and those notes.
