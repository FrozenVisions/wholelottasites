A missed-call text-back is a short message you send when you could not pick up. The person who rang a plumber, an electrician or a builder is often standing next to the leak, the tripped board or the damaged roof. They do not know if the call reached you. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This note is about the wording, and about the website that should match it. I do not sell a texting product, and I cannot promise the call becomes a job.

## When it is worth sending

You are on a roof, under a sink, or driving between Mansfield and a job in Nottingham. The phone rings and you let it go. A voicemail is easy to miss when you are on tools.

A text in your own words tells them the call reached the right business. Send it only to the number that just rang. It is a reply to that call, not a promotion to old customers, and not a list you bought. If you later want to send offers, that is a different set of rules. I am not a solicitor, so I will not write those rules for you.

You can type the text yourself when you see the missed call. That is enough for many one-person trades. Some handsets can send an automatic reply when you decline a call. Open the settings on the phone you already use, and read the message before you turn it on. I will not name an app to buy.

## What the text should say

Keep it to a few lines. Use the business name a customer would recognise from the van.

- Your name and the trade, so they know the number is yours.
- That you missed the call because you are on a job, if that is true.
- How they can reply: the problem, the town or postcode, and whether it can wait.
- When you will ring back, as a time you can keep, not “as soon as possible”.
- The same mobile number as the website, the van and your Google Business Profile.

A fair message, written in your own details, says this. Sorry I missed you. This is your name, from your business, in your town. I’m on a job. Reply with the problem and your postcode, or I’ll call you back after a time you name.

Leave out a discount you do not offer. Leave out any claim that you are the top trade in Derby, Leicester or anywhere else. Leave out a link that only opens a social page with no hours on it. Do not promise you will be there within the hour unless that is a promise you keep.

## What the website still has to show

The person may look you up while they wait, or open a link you put in the text. The page has to answer the same questions.

- The phone number in real text, large enough to tap. A number inside a photo of the van cannot be dialled. The checks are in [what to put on a plumber’s website](/blog/plumber-website-checklist/) and [what to put on an electrician’s website](/blog/electrician-website-pages/).
- The hours, including whether you take evening or weekend call-outs, and any call-out fee you actually charge.
- The towns you cover, written out. “The local area” does not say whether you will come to Lincoln, Chesterfield or Leicester. There is a shorter note for [trades in Derby and Leicester](/blog/do-trades-in-derby-and-leicester-need-a-website/).
- The jobs you do, in the customer’s words.
- A form for work that can wait, so not every enquiry has to be a phone call. Ask for a name, a number, the town and the job.

Use the same number, name and hours on the site and on your [Google Business Profile](/google-business-profile/). If those three disagree, the text and the listing will too. What else belongs on a short site is in [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## What to check before you send the first text

1. The message names your business, says you missed the call, and tells them how to reply or when you will ring back.
2. It goes only to someone who just called you. It is not a mailing list.
3. The number in the text matches the website, the van and the Google listing.
4. The site shows that number as text you can tap, plus the hours, the towns and the jobs you do.
5. You have not promised a time, a price or a ranking you cannot stand behind.
