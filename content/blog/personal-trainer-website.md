A personal trainer website is the page a new client opens on their phone before they message you. They want the sessions you actually run, whether you can take them on, and a way to ask. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield. This is for a self-employed trainer or coach. I don’t write the programmes.

A gym with a timetable, memberships and a front door needs different pages. That list is in [what to put on a gym website](/blog/gym-website-pages/). If the clients are yours, use this page. The wider checklist is [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## What a personal trainer website needs

You supply the words and the photos. I won’t invent a service, a qualification or a client. Cover services, about and qualifications, a price or a clear way to ask, booking, where you train, a few practical questions, and testimonials only when a real client gave them.

A fitness coach website can use the same pages. The sentences still have to name the people you train. A membership area, and a class grid for every hour of the day, belong with the gym article above.

## Services, in the words a client would use

List the work you take now, and leave off a programme you might add later. For each one, write the session length, what a block looks like, and what you need before the first session. That might be a health questionnaire you already use, or kit they should bring.

Say who it is for. If you only train adults, say so. If you are not a physiotherapist, and you do not give nutrition advice, say that too, so nobody arrives expecting a treatment. A small group that runs once a week can be one line of text: the name, the day, the length and the place.

## About you, and the qualifications you hold

Use the same name as your email and any Google listing. A short note on how you coach is enough. People are booking a person.

A qualification or an insurance line belongs on the page only when you hold it. Name a scheme only if you are on it. I won’t invent a registration, a number of years, or a count of clients. Use a photo only when everyone in it has agreed.

## A price, or a “from” figure, if you want one

Publish a session price, a block, a monthly arrangement, or a “from” figure only when it is what you charge. Change the page on the day the price changes. If the number depends on the person or the place, say what the message should include and when you will reply.

Don’t invent a rate to fill the gap. A website for personal trainers is still useful with no figure on it, as long as the way to ask is obvious.

## Booking enquiries

Put one next step on the home page and the contact page: book, or enquire. Link the booking system you already use. I don’t sell a separate one, and I don’t rebuild yours.

A short form asks for a name, a phone number or email, the goal, where they can train, and a day that suits. Say whether the first conversation is free, and how long it lasts. A form is a request. It holds a place only when your booking system does that. Keep the phone number in text large enough to tap, and only promise a reply time you will keep.

Card links can wait until you have your own Stripe account. If a session is on a video call, name the tool and what the client needs at their end.

## Where you train, and the towns you cover

Name the studio, the hired room, the park, the client’s home, or online. If a new client needs a gym day pass, say so. If you travel, list the towns. “The local area” does not say whether you will come to Mansfield, Nottingham or further out. If your home address should stay private, publish the town and leave the house number off.

One page of towns is enough. Use those names in the page title when they are true. “Personal trainer in [the town]” is fair for that page. “Home” is a weak title. A clear title is not a promise of a Google position. A PT website that never names a town is hard to tell from a coach three counties away.

## Testimonials, only the real ones

Publish a quote only when the client gave it and agreed you may use it, under the name they agreed. If you cannot name them, leave it off. Link to Google reviews if you have a listing. Don’t paste a star rating onto the site, and don’t borrow a line from another trainer or from a gym whose room you rent. No reviews yet means no testimonials block. I won’t write one.

## Questions to answer on the page

Answer what people ask before they book, in text, and only with rules you will keep.

- What the first session includes, and what to bring.
- How to cancel or move a session.
- In person, online, or both, and where you will travel.
- Who you don’t take on.
- Whether a block is payable up front.

## Read it on a phone, and keep it quick

Read the pages on a phone before you share the address. The services, the price or the way to ask, and the button should be readable without pinching. The number should dial. Serve the site over HTTPS, and keep photos to a size that still looks clear. Skip a video that plays on its own. Quick enough to use is the aim. I can’t promise a speed score, or a ranking.

## Your Google Business Profile

If clients meet you at a public address, use the same name, address, phone and hours on the website and on your Google Business Profile. Link the website from the profile, and the profile from the website. Point any appointment link at the same booking or enquiry page.

If you have no premises you want on a map, don’t invent a shop front. The website can still name the towns. You stay the owner of the listing. ## A checklist before you publish

1. Can a stranger say what you offer, who it is for, and how to reach you?
2. Are the qualifications and the insurance line only what you hold?
3. Is every price a price you charge, or have you said how to ask?
4. Does the booking link or the form reach you, and does the number dial?
5. Are the towns, the studio or “online” written in text, with no home address you wanted kept private?
6. Is every quote from a real client who agreed? If not, is that block left off?
7. Can you read it on a phone, over HTTPS, without pinching to zoom?
