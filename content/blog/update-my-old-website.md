If you want to update my old website, decide which job you mean. A correction is a new number, a changed price, or a service you stopped offering. A replacement is for a site that gets in the way: awkward on a phone, unclear, or so brittle that a simple change is not simple. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer.

The larger job is described on [website redesign](/website-redesign/). Run the checks below before you ask anyone to build.

## Use the site the way a customer would

Open it on a phone and note what fails.

1. Read the first screen aloud. Say what the business does, and where. A slogan that could belong to anyone does not count.
2. Tap the phone number. If nothing happens, it sits inside a logo or a photo. Retype it as text, large enough to tap, on every page.
3. Send the contact form to yourself. It should arrive, and a failure should be obvious.
4. Check the address bar. A warning that the connection is not secure means the site is not served over HTTPS.
5. Search your business name. If the result is titled “Home”, rewrite it. Each page needs its own title and a short description.
6. Read a price, a menu or the address without pinching to zoom. If you cannot, see [make my website mobile friendly](/mobile-friendly-website/).

One failure can be a text change. Several usually mean a quoted fix after a free audit, or a replacement if patching won’t hold.

## Correct what is simply out of date

Do these even if a new site comes later.

- Phone, email and hours, including a day you now close.
- A price, dish, treatment or job you no longer offer. Take it off.
- The address and which door to use, if people visit.
- The towns you travel to. “The local area” does not say whether you will come.

Use the same wording on your Google Business Profile. When the name, address and phone disagree, a customer cannot tell which copy to trust.

## Make three lists before anyone redesigns it

Copying the old brochure into a new design keeps the old mistakes.

**Still true.** The work, in words a customer would type. The hours. How to book, call or ask for a quote. Who turns up. A registration number only where the customer can check it. Don’t add a badge for a scheme you are not in, and don’t borrow reviews. If you have Google reviews, link to that profile.

**No longer true.** Former staff, dropped services, old prices, and a news page untouched for years. Leave them off the next site.

**Worth keeping.** Daylight photos of your own work. Ask before using a picture of someone else’s home or face. A logo you still use. Many firms only need a home page, services, a short about page and contact, as set out in [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

A plumber’s update needs the jobs and the towns, on [websites for plumbers](/websites-for/plumbers/). A restaurant’s update needs the menu as text, not a photo of the card, on [restaurant website design](/websites-for/restaurants/). Further notes are under [websites for small businesses](/websites-for/). Use them as a contents list for your own pages.

## Log in to the domain

The domain, such as yourbusiness.co.uk, is the address, not the website. If you already own it, log in where you bought it, or find who can, before you commission a new site. Keep that domain and point it at the new site when the new one is ready. You do not need a second address just because the pages look dated. With a new website we cover the first year of a standard domain, registered in your name. Premium domains cost extra. You pay the renewal from year two, around £5–£10 a year.

If you only have a free subdomain, or only a Facebook page, we can register a standard domain in your name. I can help connect it. I don’t need the account to be mine.
