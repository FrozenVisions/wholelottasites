A website for a gardener or landscaper has one job before you reach the gate. Someone in Mansfield, Nottingham or another East Midlands town wants to see finished gardens, whether you cover their street, and how to call or ask for a quote. I’m Chris Faulkes, a web designer in Mansfield, Nottinghamshire.

The pages for this trade are on [websites for gardeners](/websites-for/gardeners/). A close checklist for another trade is [a website for a builder](/blog/website-for-a-builder/). A general list of pages is in [what a small business website needs](/blog/what-a-small-business-website-needs/).

## What a website for a gardener or landscaper needs

1. **Services.** The jobs you take, in the customer’s words. Regular maintenance, a landscaping project and grounds maintenance are different jobs. Give each one its own section when you do that work.
2. **Areas covered.** The towns and villages you will travel to.
3. **A before-and-after gallery.** Photos of your own real work, from the same garden, only with permission.
4. **A quote form.** For work that can wait until you are finished on site.
5. **Click-to-call.** The number in real text, large enough to tap, on every page.
6. **A Google Business Profile** with the same name, phone number and area as the site.

“Home” is a weak title. “Hedge cutting in [the town]” is fair when that is the work and the town.

## Name the jobs, then show your own gardens

People type the job, not the slogan on the van. Lawn mowing, hedge cutting, borders, fencing, patios and grounds maintenance are examples. Write them as ordinary text, and only where you offer them. Leave off tree work, driveways or anything else you do not take.

A homeowner who wants a regular cut is not reading the same brief as someone who wants a patio, or a site manager asking about grounds maintenance. Say who the customer deals with, how a first visit works, and how a quote is written. If a regular round is full, say so. If you price a patio or a redesign after a visit, say that. Publish a price only when you will honour it.

If customers ask whether you take the green waste away, answer that on the page, and only if it is true. Mention insurance only when you hold it.

## Before-and-after photos of work you did

Photograph the same garden before and after, from the same angle, in daylight, and group the pairs by job type. Ask before you show a customer’s garden. If they say no, leave that job out. Phone photos in good light are enough. I crop and compress them so the page stays quick on a phone.

Use photos you took. A brochure picture, or a photo of a garden you did not work on, does not belong in the gallery. I will not use stock photos of someone else’s garden and call them yours. A handful of real pairs is enough to launch. Add more as you finish jobs. An older photo can stay when it is still your work and still the kind of job you take.

## The towns you cover

List the towns you will travel to. Mansfield and Nottingham are examples of how to write a place, and only if you will go there. Name the villages you actually cover too. “The local area” does not say whether you will come. Skip a town you will not visit, and write the job next to the place, such as hedge cutting in a town you work in. See also [trades in Derby and Leicester](/blog/do-trades-in-derby-and-leicester-need-a-website/).

## Seasonal work, and keeping the site current

Gardening changes with the month. Hedge cutting, autumn tidy-ups, spring planting and winter jobs can each have a short section. When the season changes, bring the current work to the top of the home page and take last season’s line off the top. A page that still leads with spring planting in November is out of date, even if the rest of the site is fine.

## Reviews, click-to-call, a quote form, and Google

Link to Google reviews you already have, under the same business name as the website. I don’t write reviews, and I don’t invent any. If you have none yet, leave that block off. A founding-client website includes a review starter pack: a QR code, plus review-request text and email scripts. Digital only. Ask the customer after the job, and only if they are willing.

Put the number in real text on every page, large enough to tap. That is the click-to-call. A number inside a logo cannot be dialled. Keep it above the form, and state the hours you will answer, if you have set hours. If you miss the call because you are on a job, see [a missed-call text-back for trades](/blog/missed-call-text-back-for-trades/).

The quote form should ask for a name, a phone or email, the town or postcode, whether it is a regular visit or a one-off project, and space for photos of the garden. Customers can attach those photos so you can see the job before you visit.

Use the same name, phone number and area on your [Google Business Profile](/google-business-profile/) as on the website, and add the website address to the listing. How the two should match is in [your Google Business Profile and your website](/blog/google-business-profile-and-website/). You stay the owner. Setup is free with a founding-client website. On its own, setup or a fix is £149. Optional monthly management is £99–£150. That is not a promise of a place on Google or Maps.

Read the page on a phone without pinching. Garden photos are the heavy part, so they need to be cropped and compressed, or the page is slow to open. Serve the site on HTTPS. If the only fault on a live site is speed, a form that does not arrive, or a layout that has to be pinched, start with [what to do if your website is not mobile friendly](/blog/website-not-mobile-friendly/).
